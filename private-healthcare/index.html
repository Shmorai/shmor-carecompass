Private & concierge care

Private healthcare: concierge medicine and direct primary care

Want longer appointments, same-day access or simple cash prices? Here is how private care models work in the US and how they fit alongside your insurance.

Photo: Online Marketing on Unsplash

On this page

  1. Concierge medicine
  2. Direct primary care (DPC)
  3. Cash-pay and price-transparent clinics
  4. Telehealth

Concierge medicine

Concierge practices charge an annual or monthly retainer for enhanced access: smaller patient panels, longer visits and direct contact with your doctor. Many still bill your insurance for visits, so you pay the membership on top of normal coverage.

Direct primary care (DPC)

Direct primary care practices usually charge a flat monthly membership that covers most primary care services and do not bill insurance for them. Since 2026, federal tax changes allow people in qualifying DPC arrangements to contribute to an HSA and use HSA funds for the fees, within limits. See HSA changes for 2026 and 2027.

DPC is not insurance. You still need coverage for hospital care, specialists and emergencies.

Cash-pay and price-transparent clinics

Some surgery centers, imaging centers and labs publish cash prices. Hospitals must also publish standard charges under federal price transparency rules. See how to use hospital price transparency.

Telehealth

Virtual visits are a convenient option for many conditions. Medicare’s telehealth flexibilities have been extended, and most private plans cover telehealth. Read the latest on Medicare telehealth.

Frequently asked questions

Is concierge medicine worth it?
It depends on how often you need care and how much you value access. Compare the membership fee with what your insurance already covers, and ask what is included.
Can I see a concierge doctor if I have Medicare?
Many concierge practices accept Medicare patients, but the membership fee must be for services Medicare does not cover. Ask the practice to explain exactly what the fee includes.
