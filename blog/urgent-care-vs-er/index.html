Urgent care vs the ER: where should you go?

Choosing the right place for care can save you hours and a lot of money, but in a real emergency, never delay. Here is a simple guide.

By the CareCompass editorial team · 28 September 2026 · Updated 15 October 2026 · 1 min read

Photo: Aconitum on Unsplash

Call 911 or go to the ER for

  • chest pain, signs of stroke (face drooping, arm weakness, speech difficulty), or trouble breathing;
  • severe bleeding, major injuries or head injuries;
  • sudden severe pain, confusion or loss of consciousness;
  • dental or facial swelling that affects breathing or swallowing.

Urgent care is usually fine for

  • minor cuts, sprains and simple fractures;
  • colds, flu, sore throats, ear infections and urinary tract infections;
  • mild allergic reactions and rashes.

Urgent care can help with dental pain but usually cannot treat teeth; see emergency dentist.

Costs and insurance

ER visits typically cost patients much more than urgent care or primary care visits, but federal law protects you from balance billing for emergency care in most cases (see the No Surprises Act). Check whether nearby urgent care centers are in your network, and consider telehealth for minor problems.

Frequently asked questions

Can an ER turn me away if I cannot pay?
No. Under federal law (EMTALA), hospitals with emergency departments that take Medicare must screen and stabilize anyone with an emergency medical condition, regardless of ability to pay.
