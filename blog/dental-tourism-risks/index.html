Medical and dental tourism: the risks to know first

Every year, Americans travel abroad for dental work and cosmetic surgery, often to save money. Some have good experiences, but problems can be serious and hard to fix at home.

By the CareCompass editorial team · 5 October 2026 · Updated 15 October 2026 · 1 min read

Photo: Miguel Ausejo on Unsplash

Common risks

  • Infections, including antibiotic-resistant infections.
  • Blood clots, made more likely by flying soon after surgery.
  • Differences in standards for training, equipment and medications.
  • Language barriers and unclear consent.
  • Limited legal recourse if something goes wrong.
  • Difficulty getting follow-up care and revisions at home.

Dental work abroad

Extensive treatments such as full-mouth crowns or “all-on-4” implants often need several visits and adjustments over months. Ask how follow-up will work, and be aware that some US dentists may be reluctant to adjust work done elsewhere. Compare with US options: see dental implants and veneers.

If you go anyway

  1. Follow CDC medical tourism guidance and see a travel health provider beforehand.
  2. Check the provider’s qualifications and the facility’s accreditation.
  3. Get all agreements and records in writing, in English.
  4. Plan enough recovery time before flying home.
  5. Arrange follow-up care in the US before you leave.

Frequently asked questions

Is dental work in Mexico or Turkey safe?
Quality varies widely. Some clinics meet high standards; others do not. Research thoroughly and plan follow-up care at home.
